## 1. Language Modeling Fundamentals

**Definition:** a language model is, formally, a probability distribution over sequences of tokens (section 2) — given some preceding context, it assigns a probability to what token comes next, and generation is simply repeatedly sampling from that distribution (Prompt Engineering notes' section 1's temperature/top-p discussion covers exactly this sampling step) — every architecture covered in this file, from simple statistical models to a modern transformer, is fundamentally solving the same problem: modeling `P(next token | preceding tokens)` as accurately as possible.

**N-gram models — the pre-neural baseline — Definition:** an **n-gram model** estimates the next word's probability purely from the frequency of the preceding **n-1** words appearing together in a training corpus (a bigram model conditions on 1 preceding word, a trigram on 2) — computationally simple and historically foundational, but fundamentally limited by a fixed, small context window and an inability to generalize to word combinations never literally seen during training — "the cat sat on the ___" and "the dog sat on the ___" are treated as entirely unrelated statistics under an n-gram model with no shared representation between "cat" and "dog," a limitation neural approaches (below) directly address through learned, shared embeddings.

**Neural language models — RNNs/LSTMs and their limitations (brief) — Definition:** **RNNs (Recurrent Neural Networks)** and **LSTMs (Long Short-Term Memory networks)** process a sequence one token at a time, maintaining a hidden state that's updated at each step and, in principle, carries information forward from earlier in the sequence — a genuine improvement over n-grams' fixed window, but with two structural limitations the transformer (section 3) was specifically designed to solve: **sequential processing** (each step depends on the previous step's completed computation, making these architectures inherently difficult to parallelize across the many processors modern hardware provides, C++ notes' section 14's parallelism discussion) and **long-range dependency decay** (information from many steps earlier tends to fade from the hidden state in practice, even though the architecture theoretically permits it, making it genuinely difficult for these models to reliably connect a word to relevant context that appeared far earlier in a long sequence).

**Why "just predict the next token" produces such capable behavior at scale — Definition:** it's genuinely counterintuitive that a model trained on nothing more than "predict the next token" learns behavior that looks like reasoning, summarization, translation, and code generation — the resolution is that accurately predicting the next token across a **sufficiently large and diverse** training corpus (section 5) requires the model to implicitly learn an enormous amount about the world, language structure, and reasoning patterns embedded in that text — correctly predicting the next token after "The capital of France is" requires the model to have implicitly learned a genuine fact; correctly continuing a multi-step logical argument requires implicitly learning something that functions like reasoning — this emergent-capability phenomenon, and its relationship to model scale specifically, is covered in depth in section 8.

## 2. Tokenization

**Why models don't operate on raw characters or whole words — Definition:** operating purely on individual **characters** would require extremely long sequences to represent any meaningful amount of text (directly increasing computational cost, since transformer computation scales with sequence length, section 4), while operating purely on **whole words** requires an impractically enormous vocabulary to cover every word form across a language (and still fails entirely on words never seen during vocabulary construction — a genuine problem for names, technical terms, and any other language's vocabulary) — **subword tokenization** is the practical middle ground virtually every modern LLM uses, breaking text into pieces smaller than a word but larger than a single character, balancing sequence length against vocabulary size.

**Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) and how a tokenizer vocabulary is built — Definition:** **BPE** builds a subword vocabulary by starting with individual characters and iteratively merging the **most frequently co-occurring pair** of tokens into a single new token, repeating this process a fixed number of times to reach a target vocabulary size (commonly in the range of tens to a few hundred thousand tokens) — the practical effect is that extremely common words/word-fragments ("the," "-ing") become single tokens, while rare words get broken into multiple subword pieces — this is precisely why a tokenizer trained primarily on English text tends to represent English far more token-efficiently than a less-represented language, directly affecting both the practical cost (more tokens for the same content in an underrepresented language) and effective context-window capacity (Prompt Engineering notes' section 1) for that language.

**Tokenization's practical consequences — why "strawberry" trips up letter-counting — Definition:** because a model operates on **tokens**, not individual characters, it doesn't directly "see" the individual letters composing a word the way a human reading text does — a word like "strawberry" might be tokenized as a small number of subword pieces (not necessarily aligned with syllable or letter boundaries at all), meaning a task like "count the letter r's in strawberry" requires the model to reason about character-level structure it doesn't have direct, native access to through its actual input representation — a well-documented, genuinely instructive failure mode that directly demonstrates tokenization's practical consequences, rather than reflecting some more general reasoning deficiency.

**Comparing tokenizers across model families — vocabulary size tradeoffs — Definition:** different model families use different tokenizers with different vocabulary sizes and training corpora — a larger vocabulary generally means more common phrases/words are represented as single tokens (shorter sequences for the same text, and correspondingly lower cost per unit of actual content, Prompt Engineering notes' section 16), at the cost of a larger embedding table (below) the model must learn and store — this is precisely why the same piece of text can consume a genuinely different number of tokens depending on which model's tokenizer processes it, directly relevant to the Ollama notes' section 10 discussion of chat-template/tokenizer correctness when self-hosting an open-weight model.

## 3. The Transformer Architecture

**Why the transformer replaced RNNs — parallelization and long-range dependencies — Definition:** the transformer architecture (introduced in the 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need") directly solves both of RNNs/LSTMs' structural limitations from section 1 — it processes an **entire sequence simultaneously** rather than one token at a time (making it dramatically more parallelizable across modern hardware, directly enabling the massive scale of training covered in section 5), and its core self-attention mechanism (section 4) lets any token directly attend to any other token in the sequence regardless of distance, without needing information to decay through a chain of sequential hidden-state updates — this combination of parallelizability and direct long-range access is precisely why the transformer became the dominant architecture for essentially every major LLM built since.

**Embeddings & positional encoding — giving the model a notion of order — Definition:** each input token (section 2) is first converted into a dense numeric vector via an **embedding table** — a learned lookup mapping each vocabulary token to a vector capturing (after training) something about its meaning, with semantically related tokens ending up with similar vectors — but because the transformer processes an entire sequence in parallel (unlike an RNN's inherently sequential, order-aware processing), it has **no inherent notion of token order** on its own — **positional encoding** explicitly injects this order information, adding a position-dependent signal to each token's embedding so the model can distinguish "the dog bit the man" from "the man bit the dog," despite both containing the exact same set of tokens.

**The encoder-decoder split — and why most modern LLMs are decoder-only — Definition:** the original transformer paper used an **encoder-decoder** architecture — an encoder processing an input sequence bidirectionally (each token can attend to every other token, both before and after it) and a decoder generating an output sequence, well-suited to sequence-to-sequence tasks like translation; most modern general-purpose LLMs (GPT-family, Claude, Llama) are **decoder-only** — using just the decoder half, with **causal** attention (below) — a design choice that turns out to unify essentially every task (translation, summarization, question-answering, chat) into the single, general framework of "predict the next token given everything so far," letting one architecture and one training objective (section 5) handle an enormous range of tasks without needing task-specific architectural variants.

**Layer normalization, residual connections — Definition:** a transformer stacks many identical layers (often dozens to over a hundred in a large model) — training a network this deep reliably requires specific architectural aids: **residual connections** (adding a layer's input directly to its output, `output = layer(x) + x`) give gradients a direct, unimpeded path backward through the network during training, directly addressing the vanishing-gradient problem that made training very deep networks historically difficult; **layer normalization** rescales activations at each layer to keep training numerically stable across a network this deep — neither is unique to transformers specifically, but both are essential, foundational architectural details without which training a network at transformer scale simply wouldn't converge reliably at all.

## 4. Self-Attention & Multi-Head Attention

**The attention mechanism — queries, keys, and values — Definition:** **self-attention** is the mechanism letting each token in a sequence gather relevant information from every other token — implemented via three learned projections of each token's embedding: a **query** (what this token is "looking for"), a **key** (what this token "offers" to others looking), and a **value** (the actual information this token contributes if attended to) — attention is computed by comparing each token's query against every other token's key (via a dot product, DSA notes' section 3's vector-math analog), producing a relevance score for every pair, then using those scores (after normalization via softmax) to compute a weighted combination of every token's **value** — the resulting output for each position is, intuitively, "a blend of information from every other token, weighted by how relevant each one is to this token's specific query."

**Why attention lets a model weigh relevance across an entire sequence — Definition:** this query-key-value mechanism is precisely what solves the long-range-dependency problem RNNs struggled with (section 1) — in the sentence "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big," resolving what "it" refers to requires directly connecting that pronoun to "trophy" (or "suitcase," depending on the actual sentence) regardless of how many words separate them — self-attention computes this connection **directly**, in a single step, via the query-key comparison between "it" and every other token in the sequence, rather than requiring that connection to survive being carried forward through many sequential processing steps the way an RNN's hidden state would need to.

**Multi-head attention — why multiple heads instead of one — Definition:** rather than computing a single attention pattern, a transformer layer computes **several attention "heads" in parallel**, each with its own independently-learned query/key/value projections — different heads empirically tend to learn to attend to different kinds of relationships (one head might specialize in tracking grammatical subject-verb agreement, another in resolving pronoun references, another in tracking topical relevance across a longer span) — the outputs of all heads are then combined, letting a single layer simultaneously capture multiple, genuinely different types of relationships between tokens rather than being limited to one single attention pattern per layer.

**Causal (masked) attention — why generation only looks backward — Definition:** in a decoder-only model (section 3) used for text generation, each token's attention is **masked** to only attend to tokens **at or before** its own position — never to future tokens — this "causal" constraint is what makes autoregressive generation coherent at all: at generation time, later tokens genuinely don't exist yet, so a model trained without this masking (allowed to "see the future" during training) would learn a fundamentally different, generation-incompatible behavior — this masking is also directly what makes the KV cache optimization (section 10) possible, since a token's attention computation, once masked this way, never needs to be recomputed after later tokens are generated.

## 5. Pretraining

**The pretraining objective — next-token prediction over massive corpora — Definition:** **pretraining** is the initial, by-far most computationally expensive training phase — the model is trained purely to predict the next token (section 1) across an enormous corpus of text, with no task-specific labels or human curation of "correct" behavior involved at this stage at all — the resulting **base model** (below) has learned an enormous amount of general language structure, world knowledge, and latent capability purely from this single, simple objective applied at massive scale — every subsequent training stage (SFT, section 6; RLHF/DPO, section 7) builds on top of this pretrained foundation rather than starting from scratch.

**Pretraining data — scale, sourcing, quality filtering, deduplication — Definition:** modern frontier models are pretrained on corpora spanning **trillions of tokens**, sourced from a broad mix of web crawls, books, code repositories, and other text sources — raw web-scraped text is genuinely low-quality on average (spam, duplicated content, boilerplate), making **quality filtering** (removing low-value content) and **deduplication** (removing near-identical repeated content, which otherwise wastes training compute and can cause the model to over-memorize specific repeated passages) essential, non-optional preprocessing steps — the actual composition and curation of pretraining data is one of the most consequential, and often least publicly disclosed, factors determining a resulting model's capabilities and behavior.

**Compute requirements — why pretraining a frontier model costs what it does — Definition:** pretraining a frontier-scale model requires an enormous number of floating-point operations, run across large clusters of specialized accelerator hardware (GPUs/TPUs) over a period of weeks to months — the actual computational cost scales with the product of model size (parameter count) and training data volume (tokens processed), directly connecting to the scaling laws covered in section 8 — this cost is precisely why pretraining a genuinely frontier-capable model from scratch is realistically limited to organizations with access to very large compute budgets, and why most practical model customization (fine-tuning, section 6; the prompting techniques covered throughout the Prompt Engineering notes) instead builds on top of an already-pretrained model rather than pretraining a new one.

**What a base model actually is (and why it isn't yet "an assistant") — Definition:** a freshly-pretrained **base model** is a highly capable next-token predictor, but it has learned to continue text **plausibly**, not to follow instructions or behave as a helpful assistant — asked "What's the capital of France?", a base model might continue with more questions in the same style ("What's the capital of Germany? What's the capital of Spain?") rather than actually answering, since that's a genuinely plausible continuation pattern found in its training data (a list of quiz questions, for instance) — this is precisely the gap sections 6-7 exist to close: transforming a raw, plausible-text-continuer into a model that reliably behaves as a helpful, instruction-following assistant.

## 6. Instruction Tuning & Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

**From base model to instruction-follower — what SFT data looks like — Definition:** **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)** further trains the pretrained base model (section 5) on a curated dataset of **instruction-response pairs** — examples explicitly showing "given this instruction/question, here is the kind of response a helpful assistant should produce" — unlike pretraining's massive, largely unfiltered scale, an SFT dataset is comparatively small (thousands to low millions of examples) but carefully curated for quality, since the model is directly learning to imitate the specific response *style and behavior* demonstrated in this dataset.

**Why a base model won't reliably follow instructions without this step — Definition:** SFT is precisely what teaches a model the specific **behavioral pattern** of "interpret this input as an instruction to follow, and respond helpfully and directly" — a pattern the base model has some latent capability for (since instruction-following text does appear somewhere within its enormous pretraining corpus) but doesn't reliably, consistently exhibit as its **default** behavior without this additional, deliberately-curated training specifically reinforcing it — this is the concrete mechanism directly underlying the Prompt Engineering notes' section 1 discussion of why instruction-tuning is what makes prompting work reliably at all.

**Building an SFT dataset — human-written vs synthetic data — Definition:** SFT data has historically been sourced from **human-written** examples (skilled writers/annotators crafting high-quality instruction-response pairs directly) — expensive and slow to produce at scale, but reliably high-quality; increasingly, **synthetic data** (using an already-capable model to generate candidate instruction-response pairs, sometimes with human review/filtering afterward) supplements or partially replaces purely human-written data, since a sufficiently capable model can generate large volumes of plausible training examples far faster than human annotators alone — this synthetic-data approach is genuinely load-bearing for how quickly the field has been able to scale SFT dataset construction, though the quality/diversity of synthetic data remains an active, ongoing area of methodology refinement.

**SFT's limits — why it alone isn't sufficient — Definition:** SFT teaches a model to **imitate** the specific responses in its training data — but imitation alone doesn't teach a model to reliably distinguish a genuinely *better* response from a merely acceptable one when it needs to generate something novel, nor does it directly optimize for the nuanced, harder-to-specify qualities (being appropriately cautious, avoiding subtly unhelpful-but-plausible-sounding responses, genuinely engaging with an unusual or ambiguous request rather than defaulting to a superficially-safe non-answer) that pure behavioral imitation of a fixed dataset doesn't naturally, fully capture — this specific gap is what section 7's preference-based training methods exist to address.

## 7. RLHF, RLAIF & DPO — Aligning Model Behavior

**Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback — the reward model + PPO pipeline — Definition:** **RLHF** trains a model to directly optimize for **human preference** rather than simply imitating a fixed set of example responses — the standard pipeline: first, human raters compare **pairs** of model-generated responses to the same prompt and indicate which they prefer; this preference data trains a separate **reward model** to predict a scalar "how good is this response" score matching human judgment; the SFT model (section 6) is then further trained via **reinforcement learning** (commonly PPO — Proximal Policy Optimization) to generate responses that **maximize the reward model's predicted score**, effectively learning to produce responses humans would prefer, generalized well beyond the specific examples in the original preference dataset.

**Why RLHF exists — optimizing for preference, not just imitation — Definition:** the fundamental distinction from SFT (section 6): SFT optimizes a model to reproduce specific example responses as closely as possible; RLHF optimizes a model to generate responses that **score well** against a learned model of human preference, across the full, open-ended space of possible responses to a given prompt, not just the specific examples that happened to be in a training set — this is precisely why RLHF-trained models tend to produce noticeably more helpful, appropriately-calibrated, and genuinely preference-aligned responses than SFT alone achieves, since the training signal directly targets the actual quality humans care about rather than surface-level imitation of fixed examples.

**RLAIF — using AI feedback instead of exclusively human feedback — Definition:** **RLAIF (Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback)** replaces or supplements human preference labeling (the most expensive, slowest-to-scale part of the RLHF pipeline above) with preference judgments generated by **another, already-capable AI model** instead — directly analogous to the LLM-as-judge evaluation technique already covered concretely in the Prompt Engineering notes' section 13, here applied as a training signal rather than a post-hoc evaluation tool — genuinely useful for scaling preference-data collection far beyond what human annotation alone could practically produce, though the quality of the resulting alignment is fundamentally bounded by how well the "judge" AI's own preferences actually track genuine human values and intent.

**DPO — a simpler alternative to the full RLHF pipeline — Definition:** **DPO (Direct Preference Optimization)** achieves a similar preference-alignment goal to RLHF, but through a meaningfully simpler training procedure — rather than training a separate reward model and then running a genuinely complex reinforcement-learning optimization loop (PPO) against it, DPO directly optimizes the language model on preference-pair data (chosen response vs. rejected response) using a more straightforward, directly-differentiable loss function derived mathematically to have the same underlying optimization goal as RLHF, without needing the separate reward-model-plus-RL machinery at all — this comparative simplicity (fewer moving parts, more stable/predictable training) has made DPO and its variants an increasingly common practical alternative to the full RLHF pipeline for many alignment use cases.

## 8. Scaling Laws & Emergent Capabilities

**The scaling laws — how loss predictably improves with scale — Definition:** empirical research (most notably from OpenAI and later DeepMind) established that a language model's pretraining loss (how well it predicts held-out text, section 5) improves **smoothly and predictably** as a power-law function of three factors: model size (parameter count), training data volume (tokens), and compute — critically, this relationship is remarkably **predictable** — researchers can train several smaller models at different scales, fit the resulting scaling curve, and use it to forecast the performance of a much larger, not-yet-trained model with genuine accuracy, directly informing decisions about how to allocate an enormous training-compute budget before committing to a specific, expensive full training run.

**Chinchilla scaling — the compute-optimal data-to-parameter ratio — Definition:** DeepMind's "Chinchilla" research specifically established that many earlier large models were meaningfully **undertrained** relative to their parameter count — for a **fixed compute budget**, there's an optimal balance between model size and training data volume, and prior models had generally been made too large relative to the amount of data they were actually trained on — the practical, field-changing conclusion was that a **smaller** model trained on **proportionally more data** (following the Chinchilla-optimal ratio) can match or exceed a larger model's performance for the same total training compute cost — directly explaining why the field's subsequent model releases generally shifted toward training smaller models on much larger datasets than the earlier scaling trend had used.

**Emergent capabilities — abilities that appear only past a certain scale — Definition:** certain capabilities (multi-step arithmetic, some forms of in-context learning, Prompt Engineering notes' section 3) have been empirically observed to be **essentially absent** in smaller models and then **appear relatively abruptly** once a model crosses a certain scale threshold, rather than improving smoothly and gradually alongside the smooth loss curve from the scaling laws above — this "emergence" phenomenon remains genuinely, actively debated within the research community regarding exactly how real vs. measurement-artifact it is (some apparent emergence has been shown to be partly an effect of how a specific benchmark's metric is defined, appearing more discontinuous than the model's actual underlying, smoothly-improving capability truly is) — worth holding as a genuinely interesting, real phenomenon while being appropriately cautious about overstating how mysterious or discontinuous it actually is at the level of the model's underlying computation.

**Why scaling alone has diminishing, and shifting, returns — Definition:** pure parameter/data/compute scaling (the "scale is all you need" era) has, more recently, shown genuinely diminishing returns on some capability dimensions relative to its rapidly increasing cost, directly motivating the field's shift toward complementary approaches beyond raw pretraining scale alone: more sophisticated post-training (sections 6-7), inference-time computation (a model "thinking longer" at inference time rather than only being scaled up at training time, directly relevant to reasoning-focused model variants, section 16), and higher-quality, more carefully curated training data rather than simply more raw data — the field's current trajectory increasingly treats scale as one lever among several, rather than the primary, dominant lever it was often treated as during the earlier "scaling laws" era specifically.

## 9. Model Architectures & Variants

**Dense transformers vs Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) — Definition:** a **dense** transformer activates every one of its parameters for every single token processed — straightforward, but means inference cost scales directly and unavoidably with total parameter count; a **Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)** architecture instead contains many separate "expert" sub-networks within certain layers, with a learned **router** dynamically selecting only a small subset of experts (commonly 1-2 out of many) to actually process each individual token — this lets a model have a very large **total** parameter count (and correspondingly large learned capacity) while keeping the **active** parameter count — and therefore the actual inference compute cost per token — much smaller, a genuinely significant efficiency technique increasingly used in frontier model architectures specifically to decouple total model capacity from per-token inference cost.

**Context window extension techniques (RoPE scaling, brief) — Definition:** a model's context window (Prompt Engineering notes' section 1) is fundamentally bounded by the positional encoding scheme (section 3) it was trained with — **RoPE (Rotary Position Embeddings)**, a widely-adopted modern positional encoding approach, has specific mathematical properties that make it more amenable to **extension** beyond its original training length (via techniques adjusting RoPE's internal scaling parameters) than some older positional encoding schemes — this is part of the underlying technical story behind how successive model generations have achieved meaningfully longer context windows over time without needing to retrain an entirely new model completely from scratch at each step.

**Model size tiers and their practical tradeoffs (recap Ollama notes' quantization discussion) — Definition:** model families are commonly released across multiple **size tiers** (a small, fast, cheaper-to-run variant alongside a larger, more capable one) — directly the same tradeoff already covered concretely in the Ollama notes' section 3 for locally-runnable open-weight models, and equally applicable to hosted models: a smaller model variant is faster and cheaper per request but less capable on genuinely difficult tasks (Prompt Engineering notes' section 16's cost/capability tradeoff, Ollama notes' section 9's discussion of small local models' agentic limitations) — the practical guidance, consistent across both the local and hosted context, is matching a task's genuine difficulty to the smallest/cheapest model tier that reliably handles it, rather than defaulting to the largest available tier for every request regardless of actual need.

**Open-weight vs closed/API-only models — what's actually disclosed — Definition:** directly building on the Ollama notes' section 3 discussion of what "open-weight" actually means — a **closed/API-only** model (accessible only through a hosted provider's API, with no downloadable weights at all) discloses even less than an open-weight model typically does about its exact architecture, training data composition, and training methodology — providers of closed models generally publish a "model card" or system card describing capabilities, limitations, and safety evaluations at a summary level, without full architectural/training transparency — this genuine opacity is a real, worth-acknowledging tradeoff against closed models' typically greater capability and the provider's ability to apply centralized safety/alignment updates (section 7) without requiring every downstream user to retrain or re-fine-tune anything themselves.

## 10. The KV Cache & Inference Mechanics

**Autoregressive generation — why token N+1 doesn't require recomputing tokens 1 through N — Definition:** generating text **autoregressively** means producing one token at a time, each new token conditioned on every token generated so far (section 1) — naively, generating token 100 would require recomputing the full self-attention computation (section 4) over all 99 preceding tokens from scratch; but because causal attention (section 4) means each token's key/value representations, once computed, **never change** based on what comes after it, those key/value vectors can be **cached and reused** rather than recomputed on every subsequent generation step.

**The KV cache — what it stores and why it matters — Definition:** the **KV (Key-Value) cache** stores exactly these already-computed key and value vectors (section 4) for every token generated so far — when generating the next token, the model only needs to compute the query/key/value for that **one new token**, then use the cached keys/values from every prior token (rather than recomputing them) to complete the attention computation — this optimization is precisely what makes autoregressive generation practically fast: without it, generating each new token would require redoing the full attention computation over the entire sequence-so-far, an increasingly expensive, quadratically-scaling cost as a generated sequence grows longer.

**Why context length directly drives memory usage (recap Ollama notes' section 10) — Definition:** the KV cache's size grows directly with sequence length (more tokens generated/included in context means more cached key/value vectors to store) — this is the precise mechanism directly underlying the Ollama notes' section 10 observation that a larger configured context window increases memory usage even for a shorter actual conversation: memory is reserved for the KV cache up to the configured maximum context length, not only for the conversation's current, actual length — understanding the KV cache mechanism is what makes that earlier, more application-level observation concrete and mechanistically grounded rather than simply an empirical fact to memorize.

**Batching multiple requests — throughput vs latency — Definition:** a production inference server (Ollama notes' section 13's vLLM discussion) commonly processes **multiple** concurrent requests together in a single **batch**, since GPU hardware achieves much higher computational efficiency processing several requests' matrix operations together rather than one at a time sequentially — this improves overall **throughput** (total tokens generated across all users per unit time) but can increase **individual-request latency** (a request might wait briefly for a batch to fill, or share compute resources with other concurrent requests actively being processed) — the same fundamental throughput-vs-latency tradeoff already covered generally in the System Design notes' performance sections, here specific to the mechanics of LLM inference serving.

## 11. Inference Optimization & Serving

**Quantization for inference (recap Ollama notes' section 3, the ML-precision angle) — Definition:** already covered concretely from the practical, local-model-sizing angle in the Ollama notes' section 3 — quantization's underlying mechanism is reducing the numeric precision used to represent a model's weights (and, in some quantization schemes, its intermediate activations too) during inference — modern GPU hardware is specifically optimized to perform lower-precision arithmetic operations considerably faster than full-precision ones, meaning quantization improves not just memory footprint (the angle already emphasized in the Ollama notes) but genuinely improves raw inference **speed** too, since the underlying hardware can process lower-precision numbers at higher throughput.

**Speculative decoding — using a smaller model to accelerate a larger one — Definition:** **speculative decoding** uses a small, fast "draft" model to quickly generate several candidate next tokens speculatively, which the larger, more capable "target" model then **verifies** (in a single parallel pass, rather than generating each of those tokens itself sequentially) — accepting the draft tokens that match what the larger model would have generated anyway, and only falling back to the larger model's own token when the draft diverges — a genuinely clever optimization exploiting the fact that verifying several candidate tokens in parallel is considerably cheaper than generating them one at a time sequentially (section 10), letting overall generation speed approach the larger model's quality with meaningfully better throughput than running the large model alone for every single token.

**Continuous batching & serving frameworks (recap Ollama notes' vLLM comparison) — Definition:** **continuous batching** (as opposed to simpler, static batching, which waits for a fixed batch of requests to all arrive before starting, and only starts a new batch once every request in the current one has fully finished) dynamically adds newly-arrived requests into an in-progress batch and removes completed ones as generation proceeds, keeping the GPU consistently utilized rather than leaving it idle waiting for a batch to fill or for the slowest request in a batch to finish — this is precisely the kind of sophisticated request-scheduling optimization already flagged as vLLM's specific differentiator in the Ollama notes' section 13 comparison, directly explaining *why* a production-serving framework purpose-built for high-concurrency scenarios genuinely outperforms Ollama's simpler, single-user-oriented design for that specific, high-throughput use case.

**Latency vs throughput — the metrics that actually matter in production — Definition:** two genuinely distinct metrics matter for a production LLM deployment, and optimizing for one doesn't automatically optimize the other (section 10's throughput-latency tradeoff) — **time-to-first-token (TTFT)** measures how quickly a user sees the very first piece of generated output (critical for interactive, conversational use cases where perceived responsiveness matters); **tokens-per-second (throughput)** measures the sustained generation rate once output has started — an interactive chat application should be optimized primarily for low TTFT and reasonably fast per-token generation; a large batch-processing job (Prompt Engineering notes' section 16's cost-optimization discussion) can tolerate higher latency in exchange for maximizing total system throughput across many concurrent requests — the right serving configuration genuinely depends on which of these two metrics actually matters more for a given specific use case.

## 12. Context Windows & Long-Context Techniques

**Why context length is architecturally bounded, and how it's been extended — Definition:** a model's maximum context length is fundamentally bounded by the positional encoding scheme it was trained with (section 9's RoPE discussion) and the computational cost of self-attention, which scales **quadratically** with sequence length (every token attends to every other token, section 4, meaning doubling sequence length quadruples the raw attention computation) — successive model generations have extended context windows dramatically over time through a combination of architectural refinements (better positional encoding schemes more amenable to extension), engineering optimizations (the KV cache and serving optimizations from sections 10-11 making long contexts more practically affordable to actually serve), and, in some cases, specific additional training explicitly targeting long-context performance.

**Positional encoding schemes and their effect on long-context performance — Definition:** not every positional encoding scheme extends equally gracefully — a model can technically process a longer sequence than the exact length range its positional encoding was originally trained/well-calibrated for, but often with degraded performance at the longer lengths (the model still runs, but its ability to *correctly and reliably use* information from very early or very late in a very long context can measurably decline outside its well-trained range) — this is the precise architectural root cause underlying the Prompt Engineering notes' section 9 "lost in the middle" phenomenon: a model's attention mechanism doesn't uniformly, perfectly attend to all positions in a very long context with equal reliability, a limitation directly traceable to how positional information is actually encoded and learned during training, not simply an arbitrary or incidental quirk.

**The "lost in the middle" phenomenon, mechanistically — Definition:** already covered from the practical, prompt-design angle in the Prompt Engineering notes' section 9 — mechanistically, this phenomenon is believed to arise from a genuine combination of the positional-encoding limitations above and training-data patterns (information relevant to a query more commonly appears near the beginning or end of a document in typical training data than buried precisely in the middle, meaning the model's learned attention patterns are less practiced at reliably retrieving mid-document information specifically) — understanding this mechanistic grounding is what makes the Prompt Engineering notes' practical mitigation (placing critical information near a prompt's beginning or end) something you can reason from first principles about, rather than a purely empirical rule to follow without understanding why it actually works.

**Context caching (recap Prompt Engineering notes' section 9, Agentic AI notes' production section) — Definition:** already covered as a cost/latency optimization from the application-development angle in both the Prompt Engineering and Agentic AI notes — mechanistically, prompt/context caching works by reusing an already-computed KV cache (section 10) for a prompt's stable, unchanging prefix across multiple requests, avoiding recomputing those key/value vectors from scratch on every single call — this is precisely why cache-friendly prompt structuring (stable content first, variable content last, Prompt Engineering notes' section 7) is what actually determines whether this optimization can apply at all: the KV cache reuse is only valid for an **exactly matching prefix**, so any variation earlier in a prompt invalidates the cached computation for everything that follows it, directly explaining the prefix-matching behavior already described at the application level in those other notes files.

## 13. Evaluation & Benchmarking

**Why evaluating an LLM is genuinely hard — Definition:** unlike a narrow, well-defined classification task with an unambiguous correct answer, an LLM's outputs span an enormous range of genuinely open-ended tasks (creative writing, reasoning, code generation, conversation) where "correctness" is frequently subjective, context-dependent, or has multiple genuinely valid answers — this is precisely why LLM evaluation requires a genuinely broader toolkit than a single accuracy metric: different benchmarks (below) each measure a narrow, specific capability slice, and no single number meaningfully captures a model's overall quality across the full range of tasks it might actually be used for.

**Standard benchmarks: MMLU, HumanEval, and what each measures — Definition:** **MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding)** measures broad factual/reasoning knowledge across dozens of academic subjects via multiple-choice questions — a reasonable proxy for general knowledge breadth, though a multiple-choice format doesn't directly assess open-ended generation quality; **HumanEval** measures functional code-generation correctness — the model is given a function signature/docstring and its generated implementation is tested against held-out unit tests, a genuinely objective, directly-verifiable measure specifically for coding capability — dozens of other specialized benchmarks exist for math reasoning, safety, multi-turn conversation quality, and many other specific capability dimensions — the practical guidance is treating any single benchmark as measuring exactly the narrow capability it was designed to measure, not as a general proxy for overall model quality.

**Benchmark contamination — why high scores can be misleading — Definition:** **benchmark contamination** occurs when a benchmark's actual test questions (or very similar variants) leak into a model's training data (section 5) — a model can then score artificially well on that specific benchmark not because it's genuinely more capable, but because it has effectively memorized the answers, directly analogous to a student who saw the exact exam questions in advance — this is a genuine, actively-managed concern in the field (careful benchmark-data exclusion during pretraining-data curation, and periodically releasing entirely new, never-before-public benchmark sets specifically to sidestep this contamination risk) and is precisely why a model's benchmark scores should be interpreted with some healthy skepticism rather than taken as an unquestionable, fully objective capability measure.

**Human preference evaluation & LLM-as-judge (recap Prompt Engineering notes' section 13) — Definition:** already covered from the prompt-evaluation angle in the Prompt Engineering notes' section 13 — at the model-evaluation level, the same two approaches apply: **human preference evaluation** (having human raters compare/rank outputs from different models on genuinely open-ended prompts, directly analogous to the preference-comparison data collection already covered in section 7's RLHF discussion) remains the most reliable ground truth for genuinely subjective quality, while **LLM-as-judge** evaluation (using a capable model to automatically score other models' outputs against a rubric) provides a faster, more scalable proxy, at the cost of needing periodic validation against genuine human judgment to confirm it remains a trustworthy proxy rather than developing its own systematic biases over time.

**Regression testing prompts across model updates (recap Prompt Engineering notes' section 13)** — directly connecting to the Prompt Engineering notes' section 13 point about maintaining an evaluation set specifically to catch silent quality regressions when an underlying model version changes — this is precisely why serious model-evaluation practice, both at the level of an individual application (Prompt Engineering notes) and at the level of comparing entirely different model releases (this section), depends on a consistent, well-maintained evaluation methodology applied repeatedly over time, rather than a one-time assessment that's never revisited as models and their surrounding ecosystem continue to evolve.

## 14. Hallucination, Limitations & Failure Modes

**Why hallucination is a structural property, not a simple bug — Definition:** **hallucination** — a model confidently generating plausible-sounding but factually incorrect content — is not merely an occasional implementation bug to be patched away, but a **structural consequence** of how these models actually work: a next-token-prediction model (section 1) is fundamentally optimized to generate **plausible** continuations, and "plausible" and "factually correct" are genuinely different properties that happen to correlate strongly for well-represented, common knowledge but can diverge sharply for obscure facts, genuinely novel questions, or anything the model's training data covered only sparsely or inconsistently — a model has no separate, distinct internal mechanism for tracking "am I actually certain of this" versus "does this continuation merely sound right," which is precisely why hallucinated content is so often stated with the exact same apparent confidence as genuinely correct content.

**Knowledge cutoffs — what a model does and doesn't know, and why — Definition:** a model's factual knowledge is fixed at whatever its pretraining corpus (section 5) contained as of its **training cutoff date** — it has no inherent, automatic awareness of anything that occurred after that cutoff, and, without external tool access (web search, Agentic AI notes' section 5, Communication notes' RAG discussions), has no way to know this limitation applies to any *specific* query unless it's been specifically trained to communicate its own knowledge-cutoff limitation appropriately — this is precisely why grounding a model's responses in retrieved, current information (RAG, already covered concretely in the Agentic AI notes' section 7 and the Ollama notes' section 8) is the standard, effective mitigation for any use case genuinely requiring up-to-date information the model's frozen training data simply cannot contain.

**Reasoning limitations — where these models genuinely still struggle — Definition:** despite genuinely impressive performance on many reasoning benchmarks (section 13), LLMs remain measurably less reliable than humans at certain reasoning categories — precise multi-step arithmetic on large numbers (directly connecting to section 2's tokenization discussion, since numbers aren't tokenized digit-by-digit in a way that naturally supports reliable multi-digit arithmetic), maintaining perfect logical consistency across a very long, complex chain of reasoning, and genuinely novel problems requiring reasoning patterns meaningfully different from anything well-represented in training data — chain-of-thought prompting (Prompt Engineering notes' section 4) measurably helps by giving the model explicit intermediate steps to condition on (directly connecting to section 1's next-token-prediction mechanism), but doesn't fully close this reliability gap for genuinely hard reasoning tasks.

**Mitigation strategies (recap Prompt Engineering/Agentic AI notes' RAG and grounding techniques)** — the practical, application-level mitigations for both hallucination and knowledge-cutoff limitations are already covered concretely elsewhere in this workspace: RAG (Agentic AI notes' section 7) grounds responses in retrieved, verifiable source content rather than relying purely on the model's internal, potentially-stale or hallucination-prone knowledge; explicit uncertainty-acknowledgment instructions (Prompt Engineering notes' section 12) reduce, though don't eliminate, confident-sounding fabrication; and human-in-the-loop verification (Agentic AI notes' section 15) remains the appropriate safeguard for any genuinely consequential decision or action based on a model's output — understanding *why* hallucination is structural (this section) is what makes clear why these mitigations manage and reduce the risk rather than eliminating it entirely, since the underlying mechanism producing hallucination is inherent to how the model generates text at all.

## 15. Multimodal LLMs

**How vision is integrated into a text-based transformer (brief) — Definition:** a **vision encoder** (commonly a separate neural network architecture specifically designed for processing images, itself often built on transformer principles adapted for image patches rather than text tokens) converts an input image into a sequence of embedding vectors in the **same representational space** the text transformer already operates in (section 3) — these image-derived embeddings are then interleaved with the text token embeddings and processed together through the same underlying transformer architecture, letting the model's attention mechanism (section 4) relate image content and text content to each other directly, using fundamentally the same core mechanism already covered throughout this file for text alone.

**Image, audio, and video understanding — architectural approaches — Definition:** the same general pattern (a modality-specific encoder converting raw input into embeddings compatible with the core transformer's representational space) extends to other modalities — audio is commonly processed via a spectrogram-based or waveform-based encoder converting sound into a comparable embedding sequence; video is frequently handled as a sequence of sampled frames, each processed similarly to a single image, with additional temporal-relationship modeling across frames — the unifying architectural insight across all of these is that once a modality is converted into embeddings living in a shared representational space, the same core transformer/attention machinery (sections 3-4) already covered for pure text handles the rest, rather than requiring a fundamentally different architecture per modality.

**Multimodal generation vs multimodal understanding — a genuinely different capability — Definition:** it's worth explicitly distinguishing **understanding** a non-text input (a model that can accurately describe/analyze/answer questions about an image, the multimodal prompting use case already covered concretely in the Prompt Engineering notes' section 11) from **generating** novel non-text output (an image/video/audio generation model actually producing new visual/audio content) — these are genuinely different capabilities, often implemented via meaningfully different underlying architectures (diffusion models are the dominant architecture for image/video *generation* specifically, a fundamentally different generative mechanism from the autoregressive next-token-prediction transformer this entire file otherwise focuses on) — a model being strong at multimodal *understanding* doesn't automatically imply strong multimodal *generation* capability, or vice versa.

**Where multimodal architecture is headed (brief, forward-looking) — Definition:** the field's general trajectory has been moving toward increasingly **unified** architectures — models natively trained across multiple modalities simultaneously from the start (rather than a text-pretrained model with a vision encoder bolted on afterward), and toward models capable of both understanding and generating across modalities within a single, more unified system — this remains a genuinely active area of ongoing architectural research and development, worth tracking as an evolving area rather than a settled, finished capability, directly analogous to how this file's section 8 already frames scaling and section 9 frames architecture generally as continuing to actively evolve rather than having reached some final, fixed form.

## 16. The Current LLM Landscape

**Major model families and providers — how to think about the landscape, not a fixed leaderboard — Definition:** the specific ranking of "which model is currently best" changes frequently enough that treating any snapshot as a durable fact is a mistake — the more durable, useful mental model is the **framework** this entire file has built: understanding the actual architectural/training levers (scale, section 8; post-training approach, sections 6-7; MoE vs dense, section 9; context length, section 12) that differentiate models, so a new model release can be evaluated critically against that framework rather than purely by marketing claims or a single leaderboard ranking, which can itself be affected by benchmark contamination (section 13) or benchmark-specific optimization that doesn't necessarily reflect genuinely broader capability.

**Anthropic's Claude model family, as a concrete, currently-accurate example — Definition:** as one concrete illustration of this landscape (verified current as of this writing, since exactly this kind of fact goes stale quickly and is worth checking against current documentation rather than trusting a fixed memory of it): Anthropic's current model lineup spans multiple capability/cost tiers within a shared 1-million-token context window for most of the family (Claude Opus 5, Claude Sonnet 5) down to Claude Haiku 4.5's 200K-token window — directly the same size-tier-vs-cost/capability tradeoff already covered generally in section 9 and concretely for local models in the Ollama notes' section 3, here illustrated with a real, current hosted-model family's actual tiering — pricing scales accordingly, with the largest, most capable tier priced meaningfully higher per token than the smaller, faster tiers, directly reflecting the real compute-cost differences underlying model size covered in section 9's MoE/dense discussion and section 5's pretraining-cost discussion.

**Open-weight vs closed-model ecosystems and their respective tradeoffs (recap Ollama notes) — Definition:** the open-weight ecosystem (Llama, Mistral, Gemma, Qwen — already covered concretely in the Ollama notes' section 3) and the closed, API-only ecosystem (Claude, and other major closed-model providers) represent genuinely different tradeoffs already covered concretely in the Ollama notes' section 13 — open-weight models enable local deployment, fine-tuning, and full inspection of the actual weights, at the cost of generally trailing the very largest closed models' peak capability; closed models generally offer the highest available capability tier and centralized, provider-managed safety/alignment updates (section 7), at the cost of the transparency limitations already covered in section 9.

**Specialized vs general-purpose models — Definition:** beyond general-purpose chat/instruction-following models, the field has increasingly produced **specialized variants** — models specifically post-trained (sections 6-7) or architecturally tuned for coding tasks, or for extended reasoning (models that explicitly generate a longer internal reasoning process, directly building on the chain-of-thought technique from the Prompt Engineering notes' section 4, but with this extended reasoning behavior trained in as a default rather than purely elicited via prompting) — the practical guidance is recognizing that a model's marketed specialization genuinely reflects real differences in its post-training data/process (sections 6-7), not merely a marketing label, and choosing a model variant matched to your actual task's specific demands rather than defaulting to a single "one model for everything" choice.

**How to evaluate a new model release critically — Definition:** synthesizing this entire section: when a new model release is announced, the critical-evaluation checklist this file's framework supports asking includes — what benchmarks is capability being claimed on, and are those benchmarks genuinely relevant to your actual use case (section 13)? Is the claimed context window's *effective*, reliable length actually consistent with its nominal maximum (section 12's "lost in the middle" caveat)? What's the actual cost per token relative to the capability gain over cheaper alternatives (section 9's size-tier tradeoff)? — this framework, built from understanding the actual underlying mechanics rather than memorizing any single, quickly-stale snapshot of "the current best model," is what remains durably useful as the specific landscape continues to change.
